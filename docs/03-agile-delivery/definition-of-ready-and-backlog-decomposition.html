Definition of Ready & Backlog Decomposition

Skill: Backlog management & requirements engineering · Competency: Turning product intent into deliverable work

1. Work hierarchy

Initiative / Goal       → quarterly business outcome
  └── Epic / Feature    → shippable capability (1–3 sprints)
        └── Story       → user-visible slice that fits in one sprint
              └── Task  → engineering step (hours to 2 days)

2. Requirements readiness review

Run this before a requirements document (PRD) or epic goes to planning. It catches gaps while they're still cheap to fix.

AreaReady when…
TraceabilityLinks to the business goal it supports; every requirement maps to a goal
ProblemStates the user or business problem and its evidence, not only a solution
Goals & non-goalsBoth explicit; non-goals prevent scope creep
Success metricsMeasurable, with a baseline and a target
Users & journeysPrimary personas and key journeys described
Functional requirementsNumbered, testable, prioritised (MoSCoW)
Non-functional requirementsPerformance, security, accessibility, compliance
Edge casesError states and boundary conditions described
DependenciesOther teams and systems listed, with dates
ClarityNo unresolved "TBD"s on the critical path; open questions have owners and due dates
FeasibilityEngineering has reviewed technical risk; rough size known; designs exist for UI changes
OutcomeMeaning
✅ ReadyProceed to backlog breakdown
🟡 Ready with gapsCan start; gaps are tracked as tasks
🔴 Not readyBack to the author with specific feedback

An AI assistant can do a first pass against this checklist (see AI-assisted PM workflows). A person still makes the readiness call.

3. Breakdown steps

  1. List the user journeys in the requirement.
  2. Slice vertically. Each story is a thin, testable, end-to-end slice, not a single layer.
  3. Check INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
  4. Add non-functional work explicitly: performance testing, security review, docs, release notes.
  5. Map dependencies. Log cross-team ones in the dependency board.
  6. Sequence the work. High-risk and high-learning items go first. Use a time-boxed spike when uncertainty is too high to estimate.

4. Story-splitting patterns

PatternExample
By workflow step"Add to cart" before "Checkout"
By business ruleStandard shipping first, express later
By data variationOne currency first, then multi-currency
Happy path firstSuccess flow, then error handling
By interface / platformAPI first, then web, then mobile

5. Story-level Definition of Ready

  • ☐Story in "As a / I want / so that" form, or a clear job statement
  • ☐Acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then form
  • ☐Estimated by the team; no bigger than half a sprint
  • ☐Dependencies identified and unblocked, or explicitly accepted
  • ☐UX/design attached where relevant

6. Team Definition of Done

  • ☐Code reviewed and merged; automated tests pass
  • ☐Acceptance criteria verified
  • ☐Documentation and release-notes entry updated
  • ☐Deployed to the agreed environment
  • ☐No open critical or high defects against the story

7. Worked example (fictional)

Epic: Self-service password reset

StoryAcceptance criteria (abridged)
Request reset linkGiven a registered email, when I submit, then I receive a link within 2 minutes
Set new passwordGiven a valid link, when I set a compliant password, then I can log in
Expired linkGiven a link past its expiry, then I see a clear message and can request a new one
Audit trailEvery reset attempt is logged with a timestamp and its outcome

Traceability check: every requirement maps to at least one story, and every story maps back to the epic goal.
